Hiro Nakamura (中村 宏, Nakamura Hirō, 1592 – c. 1648) was a Japanese merchant navigator and harbor interpreter active during the early Edo period, based in the port of Hirado in Hizen Province (modern-day Nagasaki Prefecture). The son of a Dutch- and Portuguese-language trade interpreter, Nakamura is best known as the author of the Nakamura Correspondence, a series of letters to his younger brother Kenji written between approximately 1629 and 1645, nearly all of which are believed to have perished.[2]

The most celebrated of the letters — the so-called Sake Barrel Letter — was recovered concealed inside a komodaru (straw-wrapped sake barrel) and concerns a Spanish-minted gold coin that Nakamura claimed to have acquired from Portuguese intermediaries in Manila some time between 1628 and 1631. The coin, an irregular quadruple escudo inscribed VITA ENIM PIRATUS and INSULUM PIRATUS, is believed to have passed through successive generations of Nakamura’s descendants in accordance with his instructions, though it has not been in documented private or institutional hands since the late twentieth century, and its current whereabouts are unknown.[3]

Of the correspondence, only a single letter is known to have survived; the remaining letters are believed to have been deliberately destroyed by the family in the late seventeenth century, most likely to avoid prosecution under the increasingly severe enforcement of the sakoku edicts. The surviving letter escaped destruction only because it was concealed inside the komodaru, unknown or inaccessible to those who burned the rest. The letter and its barrel resurfaced in 1987 at an estate sale in San Francisco, California, where they were sold as part of the holdings of a private collector and subsequently authenticated by paleographers at the University of California, Berkeley.[4] Following the completion of that study, the letter and barrel passed back into private hands; their whereabouts became unclear following the dispersal of that collection in the late 1990s, and neither has been available for scholarly examination since.

Early life

[edit]

Hirō Nakamura was born in 1592 in Hirado, then the principal foreign-trade port of Hizen Province. His father — whose given name is not preserved in surviving records — served as a tsūji (通詞), or trade interpreter, working chiefly with the Portuguese and Dutch factories that operated from the port until its forced closure in 1641.[2] Through his father’s profession the young Nakamura is believed to have learned Portuguese from infancy; Spanish he acquired in his early twenties during repeated voyages to Manila.

Little is recorded of his formal education. The correspondence makes a single oblique reference to brief study under “the brothers at Kogoshi-jima”, generally interpreted as a Jesuit mission school active in the region during the brief period of Tokugawa tolerance for Christianity.[2] No baptismal record has been identified, and the family is believed to have remained nominally Shintō.

Career

[edit]

By the late 1610s Nakamura was employed as a junior interpreter at Hirado harbor. The earliest documentary mention of him outside the correspondence is in the Hirado-han archives for 1622, where he is recorded as having been seconded to a Portuguese vessel sailing for Macau and onward to Manila, evidently to serve as on-board interpreter for a Manila Galleon trading run.[5] Over the following two decades he is intermittently attested in port records from Macau, Manila and Hirado.

The Manila trade

[edit]

The Manila Galleon — the annual Spanish trans-Pacific convoy connecting Acapulco in New Spain with the Spanish East Indies — brought silver, coinage and luxury goods to Manila in exchange for Chinese silk, porcelain and spice. Nakamura’s letters describe several voyages aboard chartered Portuguese pinazas operating in the southern reaches of this network.[6] He appears to have functioned as a multilingual fixer for cargo negotiations between Portuguese, Spanish and Hokkien-speaking parties, an unusually specialised role for a man of his rank.

It is on one of these voyages, conventionally dated to between 1628 and 1631, that Nakamura is supposed to have acquired the coin which would become the central subject of his later correspondence. In the Sake Barrel Letter he identifies the seller only as “Joaquim” (ジョアキン), a Portuguese intermediary whose name is not otherwise attested in the Manila trade records of the period.[3]

Return to Hirado

[edit]

Following the closure of Hirado to foreign vessels in 1641 and the consolidation of the sakoku (“closed country”) policy, Nakamura returned permanently to Hirado, where he is presumed to have lived in relative obscurity. His professional services as an interpreter had little use in a port that no longer received the ships he was qualified to board, and the correspondence from this period contains repeated, if oblique, complaints about household debt.[6]

The Nakamura Correspondence

[edit]

The Nakamura Correspondence is a body of letters written by Nakamura to his younger brother, Nakamura Kenji (中村 健次), then resident in the merchant city of Sakai. Composed in vernacular Japanese with occasional Portuguese and Spanish loanwords, the letters are believed to have spanned roughly sixteen years (c. 1629–1645) and to have covered topics including shipping logistics, family news, religious concerns and — most notably — the Spanish gold coin in Nakamura’s possession. Scholars have inferred the existence of at least eleven letters from cross-references within the sole survivor, though the full extent of the correspondence cannot be determined.[2]

The great majority of the letters are believed to have been deliberately burned by the Kenji branch of the family in the late seventeenth century. Contemporary scholarship holds that this was most likely a precaution against the increasingly severe enforcement of the sakoku edicts, under which documented connections to foreign traders could bring serious legal consequences. The sole surviving item — the so-called Sake Barrel Letter — escaped destruction only because it was concealed inside the komodaru, unknown or inaccessible to those who burned the rest. The barrel and its hidden letter passed through unknown hands before resurfacing in 1987 at an estate sale held by J. C. Morimoto & Co. of San Francisco, where they were sold as Lot 114 of the holdings of the late T. Tanaka, a private collector whose own acquisition history for the material is undocumented.[4]

The Sake Barrel Letter

[edit]

The most celebrated item in the correspondence is the so-called Sake Barrel Letter (酒樽の書状, sakedaru no shojō), conventionally dated to c. 1639. The letter was discovered tightly rolled and inserted through the wooden spigot opening of a small komodaru. The barrel itself appears to be of a later date than its contents — most likely the work of nineteenth-century descendants seeking to preserve the document — and bears, in red along the upper band, the inscription 高級清酒 (kōkyū seishu, “premium sake”), with the brand name 八海山 (Hakkaisan, lit. “Eight-Seas Mountain”) in larger black calligraphy on the body. A small red 謹醸 (kin-jō, “respectfully brewed”) seal appears at the lower left, indicating the brewery’s hallmark of quality.[7]

The letter itself is brief and concerns chiefly the coin. The full text, in the standard translation of Yamanaka (1989), reads:

If you find my family coin, do not sell it. It has special powers to keep the holder’s family healthy and safe. This is ancient pirate treasure stolen from Spain in New Spain that I found in Japan. Keep it secret.

— Nakamura Hirō, in the so-called Sake Barrel Letter, c. 1639.[2]

The reference to the coin as “ancient” is generally taken to be an instance of Nakamura’s well-attested taste for hyperbole, since by his own testimony in earlier letters the coin was no more than a few decades old at the time of acquisition.[3]

Authentication

[edit]

Following the 1987 sale, the surviving letter and the barrel that had concealed it were brought to the Bancroft Library at the University of California, Berkeley, for paleographic and material analysis. A 1989 critical edition by the historian Tomohiko Yamanaka concluded that the handwriting, paper, ink chemistry and idiom of the letter were consistent with a single early-seventeenth-century hand from northern Kyūshū, and that internal cross-references within the text were consistent with the eleven-letter body of correspondence hypothesised by earlier commentators.[2] A subsequent paper by Mukasa (1991) reviewed and broadly accepted these findings while noting that the provenance of the materials between the late seventeenth century and 1987 remains entirely undocumented.[7]

Following the completion of the authentication study, the letter and barrel were returned to the purchaser from the 1987 sale. Their whereabouts became unclear following the dispersal of that private collection in the late 1990s. Neither the letter nor the barrel has been available for scholarly examination since, and no researcher has been able to locate them.

The pirate coin

[edit]

The coin described in the Nakamura Correspondence is a Spanish-minted gold escudo bearing two unusual Latin inscriptions and an irregular flan characteristic of hand-struck colonial coinage of the period.[3] Numismatists have debated whether the piece is a genuine product of one of the New Spain mints — most plausibly Mexico City or Cartagena de Indias — or a contemporary novelty struck for the West Indies trade. No comparable specimen has been catalogued in any major numismatic collection.[8]

Description

[edit]

The obverse bears a stylised scene of two palm trees flanking a small island or shoreline, with the numeral 4 placed centrally between them indicating the denomination of four escudos (frequently called a “double doubloon” by English-speaking commentators, since a doubloon proper equals two escudos). Two small skull-and-crossbones motifs flank the central scene. The legend reads ✦ VITA ENIM PIRATUS ✦ (“for the life of a pirate”).

The reverse bears a Jerusalem cross with each quadrant occupied alternately by a skull-and-crossbones and a stylised hand or claw motif. The legend reads ✦ INSULUM PIRATUS ✦, an irregular Latin construction usually translated as “of the island of pirates”. The non-classical grammar of both legends is generally considered evidence of a non-standard, perhaps deliberately archaic, engraving by a Caribbean workshop rather than a sanctioned royal mint.[8]

Physically the coin measures approximately 28 mm at its widest point and weighs approximately 13.5 g, broadly consistent with the official weight standard for a four-escudo piece (c. 13.54 g) in the early seventeenth century.[3]

Provenance

[edit]

According to the Sake Barrel Letter and corroborated by an earlier letter dated to 1631, Nakamura acquired the coin in Manila between 1628 and 1631 from a Portuguese intermediary identified only as “Joaquim”.[2] The letter records Nakamura’s belief that the coin possessed protective qualities — that it would safeguard the health and safety of whichever family held it — a belief he attributes to the influence of Shintō kotodama (言霊, “word-spirit”) traditions on the spoken word of the original Portuguese seller.

The letter instructs Kenji that the coin is never to be sold and is to be passed secretly through the family line. By Nakamura’s own testimony in a later letter (c. 1644) the coin was concealed in a hidden compartment of a sleeping cabinet (納戸, nando) — a tradition his descendants are believed to have continued.[6]

Current whereabouts

[edit]

The coin is believed to have passed through successive generations of Nakamura’s descendants largely undisturbed, consistent with the instructions laid out in the Sake Barrel Letter. However, it has not been in documented private or institutional hands since the late twentieth century. A 2014 inquiry by the Asahi Shimbun was unable to locate the family or confirm the coin’s whereabouts; a Hirado-based attorney contacted in connection with the inquiry declined to comment on behalf of any client.[9] The coin’s current location is unknown.

Death and legacy

[edit]

The exact circumstances of Nakamura’s death are not recorded. The last datable letter in the correspondence is from late 1645; Yamanaka places his death between 1646 and 1648, citing the absence of his name from Hirado-han census rolls from the latter year onward.[10] No grave or memorial inscription bearing his name has been positively identified, though a small unmarked stone on the seaward slope of Mount Yasumandake outside Hirado has been proposed as a possible candidate.

Nakamura is occasionally cited in modern scholarship on early-Edo bilingual commerce and on the persistence of Portuguese loanwords in Hizen-dialect Japanese. The Nakamura Correspondence is considered a minor primary source for the history of the Manila trade in the decades preceding sakoku.[6]

In popular culture

[edit]

Nakamura’s name is sometimes confused with that of the fictional character Hiro Nakamura from the NBC science-fiction series Heroes (2006–2010), played by Masi Oka. The naming is generally regarded as coincidental: the writers of Heroes have stated that the character’s name was chosen for its perceived resonance with the Japanese loanword hīrō (ヒーロー, “hero”) rather than any reference to the historical merchant, whose name was at the time virtually unknown outside specialist circles.[11]
